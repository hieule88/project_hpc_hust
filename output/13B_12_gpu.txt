The serene landscape of the countryside was dotted with more than 500 bright green, mushroom-shaped dwellings. They were actually modified quonsets originally constructed for the United States military, and now housing the artists of the Stony Lake Music Festival. Built to support the festival’s needs and provide ample shelter for performers and patrons in the sometimes harsh Canadian weather, they stood in relative silence, waiting for that weekend to come.
From a distance, the structures looked like the mushrooms from Alice in Wonderland, or, as several people remarked, like a village of Jabba the Hut's death squads. Whatever metaphor one decided to use, the fact remained that, each year, the musicians of the festival would populate these structures, turn them into intimate, acoustically perfect performance spaces, and play music for the nearby villagers of the small, but historic, town of Haliburton, Ontario.
The concept of such a festival is simple: The selected musicians were to provide all of the instruments (sans percussion), thereby leaving the village’s percussionist without a kit. Given the circumstances, Cully proposed the following solution: He would rent a travel-friendly kit and bring it to Haliburton, utilizing his drum parts for the musical performances, and the drum kit for the local jam sessions. Cully has become a fixture of the festival, a village elder of sorts who does so much more than just play drums.
Even though Cully set up the basic rhythmic foundation for each and every performance, he was also expected to play an essential role in many of the other activities that went on throughout the weekend. A naturally inquisitive, easy-going, and friendly person, he has, over the years, befriended a number of the other musicians and has become involved in numerous facets of the festival, to the point where he is considered a fixture, and his presence is anticipated. For him, the benefits go beyond the obvious role of facilitating the festival’s rhythmic foundation. His empathy has enabled him to get to know each of the participants in a more meaningful way. As he explains, “I've met musicians who don't do this kind of thing—who might not have the opportunity to play the guitar while a singer plays, for instance. I feel lucky to have these encounters, and to be able to facilitate musical experiences for them.”
Unlike a typical club engagement, where the drummer's performance is largely expected to support the lead singer, at the festival, there is no lead singer. The structures are acoustically and visually intimate, and the festival's goal is to foster an environment in which the musicians can develop a stronger musical bond.
One can’t overstate the power of the festival's sense of community. Artists who are already working together in the band environment will further develop their rapport over the weekend, creating something much more personal than a normal gig. Other artists—who might not get to play together on a regular basis—will learn new music and styles as they create a sound together. There’s a kind of serendipity to it all, and the musicians seem to really embrace the opportunity to get together, outside of the performance context, to just talk, hang out, and play music.
“There's a big difference between being a pro musician and being a musician who can play professionally, which I consider to be a higher calling,” Cully explains. “The festival, which provides a pretty equal setting for everyone, is a tremendous opportunity for artists to explore and expand. One of the things I try to do is support them in whatever direction they go—to facilitate their improvisation and let them explore as much as possible.”
Another added benefit of the festival is its focus on different musical genres. At a typical gig, the drummer is expected to play rock-solid, straight-ahead time. “It’s not a show unless I'm playing loud,” says Cully, laughing, adding, “I’ve been known to fill every little opening.” As a result of this variety of musical styles, Cully has been able to play music that he might not otherwise get the opportunity to play in a more traditional gig setting. “At this point, I’ve played with all kinds of musicians, from rock, folk, and jazz, to bluegrass, Celtic, and classical. I’ve been fortunate enough to be in all of those different settings,” he says. “But I love the ability to step out of that framework and explore other styles of music. It helps me become a more well-rounded player.”
After setting up his kit each day at the festival, Cully began learning the pieces that would be played that evening. Since each artist and band was allocated the same amount of time—a duration that was often, in the early years, an hour or so—the musicians would need to select only a handful of songs to play. These sets were expected to be a little bit more cohesive than the traditional, improvisational “jam.” While much of the music was unrehearsed, it was still not like a typical jam session, where each artist played whatever they felt like playing. In some ways, the musicians at the festival were expected to try and create a more cohesive musical narrative with one another.
Cully’s role in setting the rhythmic tone for the evening’s performances was significant. Having worked with so many different musicians in the past, he was often able to take musical cues from the other performers, and shape the music to fit the specific style that would be expected. As one of the more experienced musicians at the festival, he was often called upon to orchestrate the rhythmic structures of some of the pieces, although he was quick to point out that his role was to set the mood—not to dictate. “I don't think of myself as a sideman,” Cully says. “I’m a part of the rhythmic fabric of the music, and I try to be supportive of it—to get inside what the others are doing, and to figure out how I can best serve that music.”
As an artist, one of Cully’s greatest strengths is his versatility. As he explains, “Drumming, for me, is the ability to adapt. There’s no real reason to play the same thing over and over again.” So, in order to keep himself in the game, he is constantly working to perfect his improvisational skills. “For me,” he says, “it’s the little details, the subtleties, that really matter, and the only way to become a better drummer is to expand my playing vocabulary and understand how to change my parts to suit the piece.”
To help facilitate this development, Cully recently began working with drum teacher Derico Watson. For the past year, they have worked together, and Cully says that Watson’s influence has really helped him to focus on the fundamentals and to become a better reader. “I want to be able to sight-read music and to play different styles of music,” he says, “because, to me, music is really a language. The only way to get better is to understand how to speak the language.”
The morning of the last day of the festival began with its traditional breakfast barbecue, and Cully took the opportunity to do some sightseeing in the nearby town. It was at this point that Cully realized he’d forgotten his drumsticks at the village. The two-hour round trip was not really an option, so he did the next best thing: He improvised.
Grabbing a set of bushes from the side of the road, Cully ran back to the festival grounds, and set about finding some participants to perform. And, lo and behold, he found two very willing musicians, a drummer and a violinist, who agreed to participate in a spontaneous performance. There was, however, one major flaw in this plan: The only pair of drumsticks available belonged to the violinist. It was a perfect irony that the drummer at the festival could not play drums! The situation could not have been any better for Cully, though, who was able to spend the next hour demonstrating his new skills. “Improvisation,” he says, “is the key to being a good musician, and improvisation is all about being in the moment.”
Cully’s interaction with the festival has been incredibly fruitful, as he’s been able to foster some of the most meaningful musical relationships in his life. In addition to expanding his musical vocabulary, he’s also learned a lot about himself as a person. “The festival has been very beneficial for me as an artist,” he says, “and it’s been especially beneficial for my musical development. But I’ve also become a better person for it, because it’s brought me together with so many wonderful people.”

I wouldn't say that the sentence is 'ambiguous' as much as it is 'potentially ambiguous'. As part of the general linguistic phenomenon called 'word hunting', there is a possible reading of the sentence that would equate the phrase "which led him to draw the conclusion" with 'that the butler did it'. Although grammatically correct, I don't think it is necessary, as I do not think that the reader would be confused at all by the previous version.
